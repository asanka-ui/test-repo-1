# The Voyage of Mariposa

The morning sun warmed the oyamel fir trees of central Mexico, and Mariposa stretched her brilliant orange and black wings for the first time. She was a Monarch butterfly, one of millions clinging to the ancient forest canopy, but something stirred within her that she couldn't explain—a pull toward the north.

"Where are you going?" asked an older butterfly resting nearby.

"I don't know," Mariposa admitted, "but I feel I must fly toward the sun."

And so she began her journey, one that no single Monarch completes alone. You see, the migration of Monarch butterflies is a relay race spanning generations. Mariposa would fly northward, lay her eggs on milkweed plants, and her children would continue the journey she started.

Mariposa traveled for weeks, riding thermal currents that lifted her high above the landscape. She could taste the air with her feet—a special ability all butterflies possess—searching for the milkweed that would nourish her caterpillar children. She navigated using the sun's position and an internal magnetic compass, gifts encoded in her very being.

One afternoon, crossing over a vast prairie, a sudden storm caught her mid-flight. Rain pelted her delicate wings as she sought shelter beneath a broad oak leaf. There she waited, her wings folded together like praying hands, until the clouds passed.

When she finally found a field of blooming milkweed in southern Kansas, Mariposa knew her part of the journey was complete. She laid her tiny eggs on the undersides of leaves, each one a promise of continuation.

"Fly north, my children," she whispered to the eggs. "Fly until you reach the summer lands. And when autumn comes, your great-grandchildren will find their way back to the mountains I came from, completing the circle I began."

Mariposa never saw Canada, but her great-great-grandchildren would—and they would carry the memory of the journey in their wings, just as she had carried it in hers.
