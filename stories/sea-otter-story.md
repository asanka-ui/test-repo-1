# The Otter Who Found Her Stone

In the kelp forests off the rocky coast of California, a young sea otter named Marina floated on her back, watching clouds drift across the morning sky. Unlike the other otters in her raft, Marina had a problem—she had lost her favorite stone.

Sea otters are remarkable creatures, the only marine mammals known to use tools. Each otter keeps a special stone tucked in a loose pocket of skin under their arm, using it to crack open the hard shells of sea urchins, clams, and crabs. Marina's stone had been perfectly flat on one side, smooth from years of use that her mother had passed down to her.

"It must have slipped out while I was diving for abalone," Marina sighed, rolling over to peer into the dark water below.

Her friend Kellan swam over, his whiskers twitching with concern. "Come with us to the mussel beds. Maybe you'll find a new stone there."

Marina reluctantly agreed. Sea otters must eat nearly a quarter of their body weight each day to stay warm in the cold Pacific waters—their dense fur, the thickest of any mammal with up to one million hairs per square inch, keeps them insulated, but it requires tremendous energy.

At the mussel beds, Marina dove deep, her webbed hind feet propelling her gracefully through the swaying kelp. Among the purple sea urchins and orange starfish, something caught her eye—a glint of grey wedged between two rocks.

She swam closer. It wasn't her old stone, but something better: a flat piece of granite, worn smooth by centuries of tides, with a small indent on one side that fit perfectly against her paw.

Surfacing with her prize, Marina floated on her back and placed the stone on her chest. She cracked open a crab with one satisfying *tap*.

That evening, as the raft of otters linked paws to keep from drifting apart while they slept—a behavior called "rafting"—Marina held her new stone close. Sometimes, she realized, losing something precious leads you to discover something even more special.
