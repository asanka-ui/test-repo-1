# The Sharpshooter of the Mangroves

In the warm, brackish waters where the river met the sea, a young archerfish named Nila practiced her aim. While other fish hunted by chasing prey through the water, Nila's family had mastered an extraordinary skill: shooting down insects from branches above the surface.

"You're lifting your snout too high," her mother demonstrated, positioning herself just below the waterline. With precision that came from years of practice, she pressed her tongue against a groove in the roof of her mouth and expelled a powerful jet of water. Three meters above, a beetle tumbled from its leaf and splashed into the water.

Nila was amazed. Archerfish could compensate for the bending of light at the water's surface, calculating angles that would confuse most creatures. They could hit targets up to three meters away with remarkable accuracy, adjusting for distance, wind, and even the weight of their prey.

"The secret," her mother explained, "is patience and practice. We adjust the power of our shots depending on how far the target is. A nearby fly needs just a gentle squirt, but a distant grasshopper requires our strongest blast."

Day after day, Nila practiced. She missed often at first, her water jets falling short or veering wide. But archerfish learn quickly, especially by watching each other. Young archerfish can improve their accuracy dramatically just by observing successful hunters.

One morning, Nila spotted a plump dragonfly resting on a mangrove root, its iridescent wings catching the sunrise. She steadied herself, calculated the refraction, and fired. The jet of water struck perfectly, and the dragonfly dropped into her waiting mouth.

Her mother swam over, pride evident in her silvery scales. "Now you are a true sharpshooter of the mangroves."

From that day forward, Nila became one of the most skilled hunters in the estuary, teaching the next generation that sometimes the best way to catch your meal is to bring it down to you.
